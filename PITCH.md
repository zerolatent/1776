# 1776 Pitch

## Core Message

1776 is an AI accountability agent for public rulemaking.

It reads live regulatory records, turns them into plain-English civic briefs, and shows with source receipts whether the public was actually heard before a rule became real.

## One-Sentence Pitch

1776 helps the governed keep up with government by turning dense rulemaking records into cited accountability briefs that answer the question most people cannot audit today: what changed, who is affected, and did public comment matter?

## The Hook

Most people think law is made when legislators vote.

But the rules people actually live under are often made later, inside agency rulemaking: the forms a business must file, the protections a resident receives, the standards a provider must meet, the way a benefit is administered.

That process is public in theory. In practice, it is buried in weekly registers, dense notices, legal citations, agency preambles, and Comment/Response sections that almost no ordinary person has time to read.

Now add AI.

Government, agencies, and regulated industries are getting tools that can research, draft, revise, and process rules at machine speed. But the public's accountability tools are still human-speed: a thin local press corps, overworked advocates, and residents staring at documents they were never trained to understand.

When power moves faster than oversight, notice-and-comment becomes a ritual instead of a check.

1776 closes that gap.

## 90-Second Pitch

Rules are where government becomes real.

They decide what a small business has to report, how a health program is administered, what an agency enforces, and whether a public concern actually changes the final text. But the evidence for those decisions is scattered across official notices, agency responses, citations, and legal language that most people will never read.

That was already a problem. AI makes it urgent.

If agencies and regulated institutions can produce, revise, and analyze rules at machine speed, then civic oversight cannot remain trapped in PDFs, weekly registers, and underfunded newsrooms. The public needs its own accountability layer.

1776 is that layer.

It ingests live Texas Register rule actions, reads the official record, and generates a short, source-backed brief for each rule: what changed, who may be affected, where the rule is in the process, and the flagship question: were the people heard?

For adopted rules, 1776 parses the agency's own Comment/Response record, pairs public concerns with agency responses, and checks whether the final rule text supports what the agency said it did. Every claim links back to a primary-source receipt. If the record does not establish something, 1776 says so instead of guessing.

This is not a rule chatbot. It is not a legal advice tool. It is not an accusation engine.

It is a civic accountability agent: one that gives reporters, advocates, and residents the ability to inspect rulemaking at the speed rulemaking is starting to move.

The promise is simple: if AI makes the state faster, AI should also make the state easier to audit.

## Why This Matters Now

The watchdog layer is shrinking. Medill's 2024 State of Local News report found 206 U.S. counties with no local news source and 1,561 counties with only one, leaving almost 55 million people with limited to no access to local news.

Public trust is already near historic lows. Pew Research Center reported in December 2025 that only 17% of Americans said they trust the federal government in Washington to do what is right just about always or most of the time.

At the same time, rulemaking is becoming faster, more searchable, and more automated for institutions. That is good when it makes government work better, but dangerous if the public cannot see how decisions were made.

1776 matters because it gives the outside world a practical way to keep up.

## Demo Story

Open with the live feed.

"This is the Texas Register. Every week, agencies publish proposed, adopted, emergency, and withdrawn rules. This is where public policy quietly becomes operational reality."

Select an adopted rule.

"A resident or reporter should not need to read the whole notice to know the basics. 1776 gives them the plain-English answer first: what changed, who may be affected, and why this might matter."

Show the public voice panel.

"But the important question is not just what the rule says. It is whether public participation mattered. 1776 pulls out the concerns people raised, the agency's response, and the finding from the record."

Open source receipts.

"The user is not asked to trust the AI. Every factual claim has a receipt. The source is the official public record."

Show watchlists and forecasts.

"For proposed rules, users can follow agencies or topics and forecast likely outcomes with play-money civic predictions. That helps watchdogs triage attention before the final rule appears."

Close the demo.

"1776 turns rulemaking from something technically public into something practically auditable."

## What Makes It Different

- It follows the lifecycle of a rule, not just one document.
- It focuses on accountability, not summarization.
- Its flagship output is "were the people heard?"
- It uses live public records, not mock data.
- It links every claim back to source receipts.
- It is citizen-facing, but useful to the people most likely to act: reporters, advocates, and affected residents.
- It is jurisdiction-agnostic by design; Texas is the first adapter, not the ceiling.

## Sponsor/SAM Frame

If a rule-navigation chatbot answers "what does the rule require of me?", 1776 answers the democratic follow-up: "how did the rule get this way, and did anyone listen?"

Those are complementary jobs.

Navigation helps people comply.

Accountability helps people govern.

## Slide Outline

1. **The Clock-Speed Gap**
   Government rulemaking is becoming AI-accelerated. Public oversight is still manual.

2. **Where Law Becomes Life**
   Rules determine the lived experience of laws, but the record is too dense for ordinary civic monitoring.

3. **The Missing Question**
   Existing tools explain rules. 1776 asks whether the public was heard.

4. **The Product**
   Live rule feed, plain-language briefs, source receipts, public voice findings, watchlists, and civic forecasts.

5. **The Trust Mechanism**
   Every claim carries a receipt. Unsupported claims are cut.

6. **The Democratic Payoff**
   If AI makes government faster, it should also make government easier to audit.

## Closing Line

The test of democratic AI is not whether government can move faster. It is whether the governed can still see it moving, understand what changed, and hold the receipts.
